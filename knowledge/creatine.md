# Creatine Monohydrate

Creatine is a compound the body makes from amino acids and also obtains from
foods such as meat and fish. Most of it is stored in skeletal muscle as
phosphocreatine, which is used to rapidly regenerate ATP during short, intense
efforts. Supplementing raises muscle creatine stores above what diet alone
typically achieves.

It is among the most heavily studied sports supplements, with a large body of
controlled trials behind it.

## How to dose it

Two approaches reach the same place:

- **Loading, then maintenance.** Roughly **20 g/day split into 4 doses of 5 g**
  for **5–7 days**, then **3–5 g/day** to maintain. This saturates muscle stores
  fastest.
- **Maintenance only.** **3–5 g/day** from the start, with no loading phase.
  Stores reach a similar level in about **3–4 weeks**.

Neither is better in the long run — loading only changes how quickly stores fill.
Total daily intake matters far more than the timing of any individual dose, so
taking it at a convenient, consistent time is sufficient.

**Monohydrate** is the form with the strongest evidence and is typically the
cheapest. Other forms have not been shown to be more effective.

## What it helps with

The clearest benefits are in activities limited by short bursts of high-intensity
effort:

- strength and power output
- repeated high-intensity efforts, such as multiple hard sets or sprints
- training volume that can be accumulated over time, which indirectly supports
  gains in lean mass

Effects on prolonged, low-intensity endurance work are much less pronounced.

Some people respond less than others, often because their muscle creatine is
already near saturation — for example, those eating a lot of meat and fish.

## Weight gain

An increase of roughly **1–2 kg in the first weeks** is common and is largely
water drawn into muscle cells, not fat. Anyone in a weight-sensitive sport should
factor that in.

## Safety

In healthy adults, creatine monohydrate at the doses above has an extensive
safety record in the research literature, including studies running for months
to years. The commonly repeated claim that it damages the kidneys is not
supported by evidence in healthy people; blood creatinine can rise because
creatine metabolism produces it, which can look like a kidney marker changing
without kidney function actually being impaired.

Practical points:

- Adequate fluid intake is sensible, as with any training supplement.
- Digestive discomfort is the most commonly reported side effect and is usually
  reduced by splitting doses or taking them with food.
- Third-party tested products reduce the risk of contamination, which is a
  general supplement-quality issue rather than one specific to creatine.

**Anyone with existing kidney or liver disease, anyone taking prescription
medication, and anyone who is pregnant or breastfeeding should speak to a
qualified clinician before supplementing.** The same applies to under-18s, where
the evidence base is much smaller.

This is general, evidence-based information and is not medical advice.
